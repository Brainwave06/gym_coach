Title: Shoulder Impingement

Overview:
Shoulder impingement is one of the most common causes of shoulder pain in adults and typically develops when the rotator cuff tendons become compressed within the narrow space beneath the shoulder blade during overhead movement. It often results from repetitive overhead activity, muscle imbalances, or poor posture rather than a single traumatic event. Most cases improve significantly with a structured exercise program, and surgery is rarely required when conservative management is followed.

Common Causes:
- Repetitive overhead pressing or reaching without adequate shoulder mobility
- Poor scapular (shoulder blade) control and stability
- Rounded shoulder posture that narrows the space beneath the acromion
- Tightness in the posterior shoulder capsule
- Sudden increases in overhead training volume

Exercises to Avoid:
- Heavy overhead presses, especially with a narrow grip
- Behind-the-neck presses and behind-the-neck lat pulldowns
- Upright rows performed with a wide range of motion
- Deep dips that place the shoulder in an extended, compressed position
- Any overhead or pressing movement that reproduces sharp or pinching pain

Safer Alternatives:
- Scapular retraction exercises to improve shoulder blade control
- Cross-body and sleeper stretches to address posterior shoulder tightness
- Landmine press or partial-range pressing that avoids full overhead lockout
- Face pulls and band pull-aparts to strengthen the rear shoulder and upper back
- Rotator cuff strengthening with light resistance bands, performed pain-free

General Guidance:
Recovery is generally guided by pain: exercises that cause sharp or pinching sensations should be modified or avoided until symptoms settle, while mild discomfort without sharp pain is usually acceptable. Improving scapular stability and posterior shoulder mobility is considered as important as strengthening the rotator cuff directly. Range of motion in overhead exercises should be limited or temporarily removed until shoulder mechanics improve, then reintroduced gradually. Most people see measurable improvement within six to eight weeks of consistent, appropriately modified training.

When to See a Doctor:
Seek medical evaluation if pain is severe, significantly limits daily activities, is accompanied by noticeable weakness, or does not improve after several weeks of activity modification. A physiotherapist can help identify contributing factors such as posture or scapular control and guide a safe return to overhead training.
