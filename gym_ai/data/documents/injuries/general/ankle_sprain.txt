Title: Ankle Sprain

Overview:
An ankle sprain occurs when the ligaments supporting the ankle joint are stretched or torn, most often as a result of the foot rolling inward or outward beyond its normal range. It is one of the most common lower-body injuries among both athletes and casual gym-goers. Structured rehabilitation significantly reduces the risk of long-term instability, while skipping rehab or returning to activity too early increases the chance of re-injury.

Common Causes:
- Rolling or twisting the ankle during unstable footing or uneven surfaces
- Returning to full training load before adequate healing and strengthening
- Weak or under-trained stabilizing muscles around the ankle
- Poor balance and proprioception, especially after a previous sprain
- Inadequate warm-up before activities involving quick direction changes

Exercises to Avoid:
- High-impact activities such as running or jumping in the early recovery phase
- Heavy loaded squats or lunges before regaining full pain-free range of motion
- Sudden direction-change or agility drills before balance has been restored
- Any weight-bearing exercise that causes sharp pain or a sense of instability
- Activities on uneven or unstable surfaces before ankle strength is rebuilt

Safer Alternatives:
- Range-of-motion exercises such as ankle circles and alphabet tracing with the foot
- Towel scrunches and toe raises to rebuild strength gently
- Seated or supported calf raises before progressing to standing versions
- Balance-focused exercises once pain-free weight-bearing is achieved
- Stationary cycling at low resistance, which is generally well tolerated

General Guidance:
Rehabilitation typically progresses through stages: restoring range of motion first, then rebuilding strength, and finally retraining balance and stability before returning to higher-impact or sport-specific movement. Most people can begin gentle mobility exercises within the first week after injury, with intensity increasing gradually based on pain and swelling. Because reduced balance and proprioception often persist even after pain resolves, balance training is an important part of preventing repeat sprains.

When to See a Doctor:
Seek medical evaluation if there is significant swelling, inability to bear weight, visible deformity, or if pain and instability do not improve with rest and basic rehabilitation exercises. A physiotherapist or sports medicine professional can help determine the severity of the sprain and design an appropriate, staged return-to-activity plan.
