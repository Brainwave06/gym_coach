Title: Knee Pain (Patellofemoral Pain Syndrome)

Overview:
Patellofemoral pain syndrome, often called runner's knee, is one of the most common causes of pain at the front of the knee, particularly in people who squat, run, or use stairs frequently. It occurs when the kneecap does not track smoothly over the thigh bone, leading to increased pressure and irritation behind the kneecap. Most cases respond very well to appropriate exercise and load management, and full recovery is common when training is adjusted correctly.

Common Causes:
- Muscle imbalance between the inner and outer thigh muscles affecting kneecap tracking
- Rapid increases in squatting, running, or stair-climbing volume
- Weak hip and glute muscles, which affect overall leg alignment
- Exercises performed with excessive knee flexion under load
- Poor movement control during landing or deceleration

Exercises to Avoid:
- Deep squats or lunges that take the knee well past 90 degrees of bend
- Leg extension machine work with heavy resistance, which isolates and loads the kneecap directly
- Full-depth squats performed early in the recovery phase
- Kneeling directly on the affected knee
- Any movement that reproduces sharp pain behind the kneecap

Safer Alternatives:
- Shallow or partial-range squats kept within a pain-free range of motion
- Wall sits at a comfortable depth to build quad strength with less kneecap compression
- Clamshells and side-lying hip abduction to strengthen the hips
- Straight-leg raises to work the quadriceps without bending the knee
- Cycling at low resistance once acute irritation has settled

General Guidance:
Exercise selection should be guided by pain rather than avoided altogether, since inactivity does not resolve the underlying strength and movement issues. Closed-chain exercises performed within a comfortable, shallow range tend to be better tolerated than deep or heavily loaded knee-bending movements. Strengthening the hips and glutes is considered as important as directly working the quadriceps, since hip weakness often contributes to poor kneecap tracking. Progress should be gradual, increasing depth and load only as symptoms allow.

When to See a Doctor:
Consult a doctor or physiotherapist if pain is severe, the knee gives way, there is visible swelling, or pain persists despite reducing aggravating activities for several weeks. A professional assessment can help confirm the cause of the pain and guide a safe return to squatting, running, or other knee-loading activities.
