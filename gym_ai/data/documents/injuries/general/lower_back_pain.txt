Title: Lower Back Pain

Overview:
Low back pain is one of the most common musculoskeletal complaints among gym-goers and is considered a leading cause of activity limitation worldwide. Most cases are mechanical in nature, meaning they relate to how the spine is loaded and moved rather than a serious underlying disease. A common misconception is that people with back pain should avoid movement entirely; in reality, most guidelines emphasize that staying active and gradually reintroducing exercise supports recovery more than prolonged rest.

Common Causes:
- Reduced core and trunk muscle endurance, which lowers spinal stability
- Lifting technique that loads the spine in a rounded or unsupported position
- Sudden jumps in training volume or intensity without adaptation time
- Prolonged sitting combined with limited overall physical activity
- Excess body weight altering normal spinal loading patterns
- Limited hip mobility that forces compensation through the lower back

Exercises to Avoid:
- Heavy conventional deadlifts performed with poor spinal bracing
- Good mornings and other loaded hip-hinge movements with a rounded back
- Loaded spinal flexion exercises such as weighted sit-ups
- Heavy barbell squats performed with breakdown in lumbar position
- Any exercise that reproduces sharp or radiating pain

Safer Alternatives:
- Bird Dog, to train core control without loading the spine
- Dead Bug, for anti-extension core stability
- Glute Bridge, to build hip and glute strength that supports the lower back
- Cat-Cow stretch, for gentle spinal mobility
- Walking and light aerobic activity, which is generally well tolerated and encouraged

General Guidance:
Clinical guidance consistently supports staying active rather than resting completely, since muscle endurance in the back is associated with a lower risk of pain. Core stabilization exercises targeting the deep trunk muscles are considered a key part of both prevention and recovery. Training should be reintroduced gradually, with exercise selection guided by what can be performed without reproducing sharp pain. Programs that combine strengthening, flexibility, and general aerobic fitness tend to produce the best outcomes.

When to See a Doctor:
Seek professional evaluation if pain is severe, radiates down one or both legs, is accompanied by numbness, tingling, or weakness, or does not improve with relative rest and activity modification. A physiotherapist or physician should guide exercise selection in cases of persistent or recurring low back pain, since some exercises may need to be individually modified or temporarily avoided based on the specific cause.
